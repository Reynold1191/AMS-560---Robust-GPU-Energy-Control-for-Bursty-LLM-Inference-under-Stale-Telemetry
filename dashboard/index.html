What this project is about, explained with a restaurant kitchen

An LLM server is like a busy kitchen. Requests are orders, and the GPU is the cooking crew. A manager decides how hard the kitchen works. Working harder serves food faster but burns more gas and electricity. This project is about a manager who has to make that call while looking at a status board that is out of date.

The cast

Every part of the real system has a kitchen counterpart.

🧾
Order= one LLM request (a prompt that needs an answer)
📌
Ticket rail= the request queue of orders waiting to be cooked
👩‍🍳
Kitchen crew= the GPU. It cooks 16 orders per round (batch size 16)
🔥
Stove setting= the GPU clock (180 to 2122 MHz). Higher is faster but uses more power
💡
Gas & electricity bill= energy, measured as joules per output token (J/token)
⏱️
Service promise= the SLO: every dish leaves the kitchen within 5 s
🎟️
Allowed late dishes= the error budget: at most 5 % may be late. Burn rate is how fast we use it up
🖥️
Status board= telemetry: power, clock, orders waiting, recent delivery times (P95)
🕰️
Board lag= stale telemetry: the board shows the kitchen as it was 0, 0.5, 1.25 or 2.5 s ago
🧑‍💼
Manager= the controller. Every second it sets the stove for the next second
🍔
Lunch rush= the sharp burst: 4× the normal orders for 20 s. Also steady and slowly growing ramp days
📖
Recipe= the LLM itself. The manager never changes it, so answers are identical. Only the speed changes

The problem: a manager reading an old board

  1. At 12:00:00 a lunch rush starts and orders pile onto the rail.
  2. The board only refreshes with a lag. At 12:00:02 it still shows "rail almost empty, dishes going out fast".
  3. A manager who trusts the board says "we're quiet, turn the stove down to save gas", at exactly the wrong moment.
  4. By the time the board shows the pile-up, there is a backlog. The kitchen needs several rounds to clear it and many dishes are late.
  5. After the rush the opposite happens. The board's "late dishes in the last minute" number stays bad for up to 60 s, so the manager keeps the stove on full with an empty rail and wastes gas.

The two failure modes are reacting late, which causes SLO misses, and calming down late, which wastes energy. The staler the board, the worse both get.

Our two managers

Naive manager naive_slo

Looks at the board and believes it is live. If recent dishes were slow, turn up. If the rail is empty and dishes are fast, turn down.

Robust manager robust

1. Reads the timestamp. "This board is 2.5 s old, and a stove change takes 0.7 s to bite, so I plan 3.2 s ahead."

2. Watches the rail, not the average. The rail growing is an early sign. A one-minute delivery-time average is a late one. When the rail is surging it projects the pile-up forward and turns up before the board shows the slowdown.

3. Ignores stale bad news. If the rail has been empty for the whole lag window, it knows the bad average is history and turns down.

Both managers share one safety rule, the capacity floor: never set the stove so low that the kitchen serves fewer orders than 1.25 × the order rate it sees. So the comparison is about the extra judgement only.

The research question

If the manager knows how old the board is and plans for it, can the kitchen use less energy and keep its 5-second promise better, when the board lags and lunch rushes hit?

How we test itA real RTX 5060 laptop GPU serving Qwen2.5-0.5B. Each 2-minute run has 10 s warm-up, 40 s normal, a 20 s rush and 50 s recovery. Same orders for both managers (paired seed).
What we varyManager (naive / robust, plus 2 ablations), board lag (0 / 500 / 1250 / 2500 ms) and type of day (steady / ramp / sharp burst). That is 35 runs per seed.
What we measureEnergy per output token, the share of orders that broke the 5 s promise (overall and during the rush), P95 latency, and how often the stove was changed.

What happened in the kitchen (one example day: attempt_13, seed 41)

Rush with a 2.5 s-old boardThe naive manager was late on 36.8 % of rush orders (53 of 144). The robust manager was late on none of them (0 of 144). With a fresh board naive was late on 6.3 %, robust on 0 %.
Gas bill on rush daysRobust used 6–10 % less energy than naive at every board lag, mostly by turning the stove down after the rush (25 % of the day on full flame instead of 57 %). It used 15–18 % less than running full blast all day.
Quiet and ramp daysBoth managers kept the promise (at most one late order per day). On quiet days the gas bill is the same; on ramp days robust spends a little more (+1 to +5 %), because it prepares for a rise the naive manager copes with anyway.

Note: this is one showcase replicate out of nine (3 sessions × 3 seeds), picked because it shows the typical pattern cleanly. Did it happen again? (3-seed repeats, attempt_13 + 14 + 15)

Rush, every board lagOver 9 paired replicates (3 sessions × 3 seeds, 1331 rush orders per cell) the naive manager was late on 13.3 → 20.1 → 27.6 → 49.4 % of rush orders as the board lag grew 0 → 2.5 s. The robust manager: 1.9 → 0.7 → 2.5 → 5.6 %.
Gas bill on rush daysRobust was cheaper in 35 of 36 paired rush runs, −5.9 % on average. The chance of that by luck is about 1 in a billion. Robust still saves 15–28 % against running full blast.
The honest costOn ramp days with a 2.5 s-old board robust spends +2.7 % more gas: it plans for a rise the naive manager copes with anyway. On the busiest seed robust also starts to run late at 1.25–2.5 s lag (8–26 %), still far better than naive (65–95 %).

Does knowing how old your telemetry is make GPU energy control safer?

A controller picks the GPU clock every second to save energy while keeping LLM request latency under an SLO. It only sees telemetry that is d ms old. We compare a reactive controller (naive) against one that knows the telemetry age, forecasts over it and ignores lagging latency signals (robust), on a real RTX 5060 Laptop GPU.

Experiment setup

How one run works

  1. Load generator sends Poisson requests: steady, ramp or a 4× sharp burst.
  2. LLM worker serves them in batches of 16 (Qwen2.5-0.5B, long-context prompts).
  3. Telemetry (power, clock, queue, P95, error-budget burn, arrival rate) goes through a DelayBuffer, so the controller sees data that is d ms old.
  4. Controller picks one of 5 clocks (180 to 2122 MHz) every 1 s. nvidia-smi locks it.
  5. Metrics integrate NVML power into joules and record every request's latency against the 5 s SLO.

How to read it

  • Arrival rate: the solid line is the true 10 s rate. The dotted line is what the controller saw (delayed).
  • Queue: solid is true, dotted is observed. At 2500 ms delay the controller sees a queue that is 2.5 s old.
  • Clock: the clock each controller chose. Naive often stays at 2122 MHz after the burst because the 60 s P95 window still remembers it. Robust drops back when there is no backlog.
  • Latency: one dot per request, plotted at its arrival time. Points above the red SLO line are misses.
